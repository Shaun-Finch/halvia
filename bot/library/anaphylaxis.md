# Anaphylaxis: emergency treatment

Source: based on Resuscitation Council UK, Emergency treatment of anaphylaxis: guidelines for healthcare providers (2021). Summary written by Halvia. Check the current guideline and your local policy.

## Recognising anaphylaxis
Anaphylaxis is likely when there is a sudden onset and rapid progression of symptoms, life-threatening Airway and/or Breathing and/or Circulation problems, and usually skin or mucosal changes such as flushing, urticaria or angioedema. Skin changes can be subtle or absent in up to a fifth of reactions. Airway problems include throat or tongue swelling, hoarse voice and stridor. Breathing problems include shortness of breath, wheeze, tiring, confusion from hypoxia, cyanosis and respiratory arrest. Circulation problems include signs of shock, tachycardia, hypotension, decreased consciousness and cardiac arrest.

## First steps
Call for help early. Remove the trigger if possible, for example stop an infusion. Lie the patient flat, with legs raised if hypotensive; let them sit up if breathing is difficult. Do not let them stand or walk. Pregnant patients should lie on their left side. Assess with the ABCDE approach.

## Intramuscular adrenaline
Give intramuscular (IM) adrenaline 1 mg/mL (1 in 1000) into the anterolateral thigh. Adult and child over 12 years: 500 micrograms IM (0.5 mL). Child 6 to 12 years: 300 micrograms IM (0.3 mL). Child 6 months to 6 years: 150 micrograms IM (0.15 mL). Child under 6 months: 100 to 150 micrograms IM (0.1 to 0.15 mL). Repeat the IM dose after 5 minutes if there is no improvement. An adrenaline auto-injector can be used if that is what is available.

## Further treatment
Establish airway, give high-flow oxygen and attach monitoring. Give a rapid IV fluid bolus of crystalloid: 500 to 1000 mL in adults, 10 mL/kg in children. If respiratory or cardiovascular problems continue despite two doses of IM adrenaline, this is refractory anaphylaxis: get expert help early and follow the refractory anaphylaxis algorithm, which uses a low-dose adrenaline infusion given by experienced staff with monitoring. Never give undiluted adrenaline 1 mg/mL intravenously.

## Antihistamines and steroids
Antihistamines are not a first-line treatment for anaphylaxis; they may be used for skin symptoms once the patient is stable. Routine use of corticosteroids for the emergency treatment of anaphylaxis is no longer advised.

## Mast cell tryptase and follow-up
Take a blood sample for mast cell tryptase as soon as possible after starting treatment, and a second sample ideally within 1 to 2 hours (no later than 4 hours) from the onset of symptoms. Refer all patients with suspected anaphylaxis to an allergy service, give advice and, where indicated, adrenaline auto-injectors with training.

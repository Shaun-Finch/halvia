# Black eye: aftercare advice for patients

Source: based on general first-aid guidance for a periorbital haematoma (black eye) from public health information, written to match the Halvia patient explainer video. Summary written by Halvia. This is general information, not a diagnosis. Follow the advice of the clinician who examined you.

## What a black eye is
A black eye is bruising and swelling around the eye, usually after a knock to the face. Blood collects under the skin, which turns it dark red, blue or purple, then green and yellow as it heals. Most black eyes get better on their own in two to three weeks.

## Looking after it at home
Hold a cold compress, such as a bag of frozen peas wrapped in a clean tea towel, gently against the area for about 10 to 20 minutes at a time, several times a day for the first day or two. Do not put ice directly on the skin and do not press on the eye itself. Rest, and sleep with your head slightly raised to help the swelling go down. Simple painkillers such as paracetamol can help; check the leaflet and ask a pharmacist if you are not sure which is right for you. After a couple of days, a warm compress can help the bruise fade.

## Get urgent help if
Go to A&E or call 999 if you have: changes in your vision, such as blurred or double vision or loss of vision; blood visible in the coloured part of the eye or on the surface of the eye; severe pain in the eye or difficulty moving the eye; both eyes bruised, or bruising behind the ear; clear fluid or blood coming from the nose or ears; a severe headache, being sick, drowsiness, confusion or any other signs of a head injury; or numbness in the cheek, teeth or lip. Also seek urgent advice if the injury was caused by something sharp or travelling fast, or if something might be stuck in the eye.

## Speak to your GP or NHS 111 if
The swelling or pain is getting worse after the first two days, the bruise has not improved after two weeks, or you are worried for any other reason.

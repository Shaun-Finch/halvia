# Delirium in hospital

Source: based on NICE guideline CG103, Delirium: prevention, diagnosis and management in hospital and long-term care (updated 2023), and the 4AT assessment test. Summary written by Halvia. Follow your local delirium pathway.

## Who is at risk
People at higher risk of delirium include those aged 65 or over, people with cognitive impairment or dementia, people with a current hip fracture and people who are severely ill. Delirium can be hyperactive (agitated), hypoactive (quiet and withdrawn, easily missed) or mixed.

## Spotting delirium
Look for recent changes or fluctuations in behaviour: cognitive function (worse concentration, slow responses, confusion), perception (hallucinations), physical function (reduced mobility, falls, restlessness), and social behaviour (withdrawal, changes in communication, mood or attitude). Use a validated tool such as the 4AT: a score of 4 or more suggests possible delirium, 1 to 3 suggests possible cognitive impairment.

## Find and treat the cause
Delirium usually has one or more physical causes. Look for infection, pain, dehydration, constipation, urinary retention, hypoxia, electrolyte and glucose disturbance, alcohol or drug withdrawal, and medicines (for example opioids, anticholinergics, sedatives and steroids). Review the medication chart.

## Supportive care
Use non-drug measures first: reorientation, clear communication, a familiar person present, glasses and hearing aids, good lighting with a visible clock, avoiding unnecessary moves between wards, sleep hygiene, mobilisation, nutrition and hydration.

## Medicines
If a person with delirium is distressed or a risk to themselves or others, and non-drug measures have not worked, consider short-term haloperidol at the lowest effective dose, usually for 1 week or less, and with caution. Avoid antipsychotics in Parkinson's disease or dementia with Lewy bodies. Avoid benzodiazepines unless treating alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal.

# Venous thromboembolism: prevention, DVT and PE

Source: based on NICE guideline NG89, Venous thromboembolism in over 16s: reducing the risk of hospital-acquired DVT or PE (updated 2019), and NG158, Venous thromboembolic diseases: diagnosis, management and thrombophilia testing (updated 2023). Summary written by Halvia. Follow your local VTE policy.

## Risk assessment
Assess all adults admitted to hospital for VTE and bleeding risk as soon as possible after admission, or by the time of the first consultant review, using a national tool such as the Department of Health VTE risk assessment tool. Reassess at consultant review and whenever the clinical situation changes.

## Prevention
Offer pharmacological prophylaxis, usually low molecular weight heparin, to people whose risk of VTE outweighs their risk of bleeding, starting as soon as possible and within 14 hours of admission. Consider mechanical prophylaxis (anti-embolism stockings or intermittent pneumatic compression) if pharmacological prophylaxis is contraindicated, unless there is a reason not to use it. Encourage mobilisation and hydration.

## Suspected DVT
Use the two-level DVT Wells score. If DVT is likely (2 points or more), offer a proximal leg vein ultrasound within 4 hours, or a D-dimer and interim anticoagulation if the scan can't be done within 4 hours. If DVT is unlikely (1 point or less), offer a D-dimer test.

## Suspected PE
Use the two-level PE Wells score. If PE is likely (more than 4 points), offer a CT pulmonary angiogram (CTPA) immediately, with interim anticoagulation if there will be a delay. If PE is unlikely (4 points or less), offer a D-dimer; if it is positive, offer CTPA. In people with suspected PE and haemodynamic instability, seek urgent senior help: thrombolysis may be needed.

## Treatment
Offer apixaban or rivaroxaban as first-line anticoagulation for confirmed DVT or PE for most adults, checking for contraindications and interactions.
